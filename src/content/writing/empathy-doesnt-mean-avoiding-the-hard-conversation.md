There was a part of my personality that took me longer to understand as a manager than I expected it to. I tend to give people the benefit of the doubt. Someone misses a commitment — there is probably a reason. Someone isn't performing well — maybe there is something going on that I don't know about. Someone is struggling with a responsibility — maybe they need more context or support.

As a manager, that instinct was useful. People don't come to work as perfectly functioning machines. Things happen. Sometimes what looks like a performance problem is actually a communication problem, a role problem, or something happening outside work. So I tried to understand before judging.

The problem was when understanding slowly turned into making too many exceptions.

## Understanding someone doesn't remove accountability

This was one of the harder things for me to learn. You can understand why someone missed something and still expect them to improve. You can care about someone and still give difficult feedback. You can know that someone is having a difficult time and still have to make a decision about their performance. Those aren't contradictions.

Earlier in my management career, I sometimes mixed empathy with responsibility for making the situation comfortable for everyone. That's not really the manager's job. The job is to make the situation clear: what happened, what was expected, what needs to change, what support is available, and what happens if it doesn't change. The more people I managed, the more important that clarity became.

(I've written elsewhere about a narrower version of this — one specific hard call, performance ratings. This is closer to the general shape underneath it: [Sometimes the manager has to take the hit](/writing/manager-takes-the-hit).)

## The distance that comes with leadership

I also had to learn to create some distance. Not personal distance. Decision-making distance.

When you're close to a team, you naturally know the context behind everyone's work — who stayed late, who helped someone, who had a difficult week, who has been doing good work quietly. That context matters. But if you carry all of it into every decision, it becomes difficult to be consistent. As the organization grows, you can't make every decision based on the last conversation you had with someone.

That became particularly important when I had to be involved in difficult organizational decisions, including layoffs. I've had to carry that responsibility at different points in my leadership career. Those are not conversations you forget, and neither do the people on the other side of them.

You can explain the business context, the reasons behind the decision, and what you did to make the process as fair as possible. But once you're the person delivering the message, you also have to accept that people may see you differently afterwards.

Leadership isn't a popularity contract. Sometimes you have to make or communicate a decision that people don't like, while still treating them with respect. You can hope people understand why you did it. You can't expect them to feel the same way about you afterwards.

## Empathy doesn't mean avoiding the uncomfortable conversation

Those experiences changed how I thought about empathy. Earlier, I thought empathy mostly meant understanding how someone felt. Later, I realised that sometimes empathy means being willing to have the uncomfortable conversation clearly, rather than trying to make it easier by avoiding it. The person in front of you may not like the decision. They may never agree with it. But they deserve honesty and respect while you're communicating it.

That's probably one of the bigger changes in how I think about management now. I still try to understand people before judging them. That hasn't changed, and I don't want it to.

What changed is that understanding is no longer where the job stops for me. It's where it starts.
