title: We hired interns, but we hadn't built a program for them
---

Initially, we hired interns and assigned them to frontend, backend or QA
based on the initial screening. The idea was straightforward: they would
spend six months with the team, and we'd evaluate whether they were
ready to convert to full-time roles.

The first few batches didn't convert as many people as we'd hoped. It
took a closer look to understand why.

We had placed interns with experienced engineers, but we hadn't really
planned what they were supposed to learn over those six months. Seniors
were busy with their own work, so an intern might get a small task and a
short explanation, then be expected to figure out the rest. That can
work for someone who already knows the product and the team's habits. It
isn't a great learning plan for someone just starting out.

## Give the first few months some structure

We changed the program so the first three months followed a learning
plan. Instead of relying on whatever task a team happened to have
available, interns would work through topics that helped them understand
the product and the technologies they would need to use.

From the fourth month, we started bringing them closer to the team's
day-to-day work. They could join stand-ups, look at bugs reported by QA
and help investigate and fix them. The aim was to move from learning in
isolation to contributing to the actual product, with support from the
team.

That gave the internship a clearer progression. There was a period for
building context, followed by opportunities to apply what they had
learned.

## The mentor mattered more than we'd accounted for

The other change came out of my one-to-ones with the interns.

It was clear that the experience varied depending on who was mentoring
them. Some had mentors who made time to explain things and guide them.
Others weren't getting the same level of support. The interns were going
through the same program on paper, but their day-to-day experience could
be quite different.

We decided to rotate mentors every month. That gave each intern the
chance to learn from more than one person instead of being dependent on
a single mentor's availability or style.

We also documented feedback from both sides: the intern's experience
with the mentor, and the mentor's feedback on the intern. That helped us
understand what was working and where either side needed more support.

## An internship needs more than a task list

The original setup wasn't unreasonable. We had teams that needed help,
people who could mentor, and a six-month window to assess interns. What
was missing was a deliberate structure connecting those things.

A senior engineer can't be expected to invent an internship program on
top of a full workload. And an intern shouldn't have to rely on luck to
get a useful learning experience.

The learning plan gave the interns a clearer path. The mentor rotation
gave them different perspectives and made the experience less dependent
on one person. The regular feedback gave us a way to notice problems
instead of waiting until the end of six months to discover that the
arrangement hadn't worked.

I wouldn't say a structure like this removes every challenge. People
learn at different speeds, and teams still need to make time for
mentoring. But it gave us a much better starting point than simply
placing an intern in a team and hoping things worked out.
