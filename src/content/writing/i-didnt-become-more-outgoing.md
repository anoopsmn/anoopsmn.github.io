For most of my career, I was comfortable being the person behind the scenes. I didn't look for attention. I was quite happy if someone else presented the work, and I never felt the need to speak in a meeting just because there was silence. If there was a problem to solve, I'd rather spend the time solving it than talking about how I was solving it. That worked reasonably well for a long time.

But as my responsibilities grew, I slowly realised that leadership comes with a part I hadn't really considered:

being visible.

Not being loud. Not being charismatic. Just being visible enough that people know what you're doing, why you're doing it, and where you stand.

## I was never the loud person in the room

Early in my career, I associated good work with being good at the work itself. Write good code. Solve difficult problems. Help people when they get stuck. Get the release out.

As I moved into architecture and then management, that changed gradually. There were more people, more decisions and more things happening outside my immediate team. But my instinct stayed the same: listen first, speak when necessary, and don't make noise just to be noticed. For a while, I thought that was enough. In many situations, it was.

But leadership has a visibility component that I hadn't fully appreciated. When you're responsible for a team, people sometimes need to know where you stand. When something goes wrong, someone needs to explain what happened. When a decision is unpopular, someone needs to stand behind it.

Being quiet is fine. Being invisible isn't always.

## The difficult part is managing in both directions

One thing that became much clearer as I moved into management was how different the view can be from either side. You know what your manager thinks about a person. You also know what that person thinks about management. And you're somewhere in between.

If you lean too much towards what management wants, your team can feel that you're not representing them anymore. If you lean too much towards your team, management can feel that you don't understand the direction they are trying to take. Neither side necessarily sees the full picture, and you're expected to deal with both.

I've seen this play out in fairly ordinary things — priorities, delivery expectations, performance discussions, promotions, process changes. There is rarely a perfect position in the middle. You make a decision, explain it to one side, then explain it again from a different angle to the other side. And sometimes neither side is particularly happy. That's part of the job.

## Then there is the problem of being visible

This became more interesting for me because I'm not naturally someone who sells himself well. Some people can spend thirty minutes explaining everything they've accomplished in a way that makes it immediately obvious what they contributed. That's not naturally how I work. I'd rather show the work.

So over time I started relying more on evidence. What changed? What did we ship? How did the team grow? What problems did we solve? What improved? What did people say? What happened before and after?

That became important not just for my own career, but also for the people working with me. Because if the work isn't visible, it is surprisingly easy for good work to disappear.

## I didn't need to become someone else

I still prefer smaller conversations. I still don't feel the need to speak in every meeting. I'm still much more comfortable discussing an actual problem than talking about myself. I don't think any of that needs fixing.

What changed is that I understand the responsibility differently now. If I'm leading a team and something needs to be communicated, I'll communicate it. If the team needs context, I'll provide it. And if I need to explain what I have accomplished, I try to bring the evidence rather than rely on how well I can sell myself.

I still prefer being behind the scenes most of the time. These days, I just know when I can't stay there.
